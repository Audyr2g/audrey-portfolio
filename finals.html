Lab report, Word document, 5 pages

This report prepares the chunking experiment. It records my dataset, the Member 3 corpus of 126 SEC lending records, and the three Member 3 test questions with their expected answers and exact evidence pages. It names the final model, aisingapore/Llama-SEA-LION-v3-8B-IT, and gives the full prompt I wrote for it.

The prompt adds four rules for this dataset: date and type every record used, treat a registry listing as evidence of recording only, never attribute an impersonation advisory to the registered company, and say which part of a question the evidence does not cover. At the 1000/200 baseline the model answered the difficult Tala question correctly and refused the Peso2you question instead of guessing, because page 46 was never retrieved.

Chunk Size and Overlap in a FAISS-Based Retrieval Pipeline

Title
Chunk Size and Overlap in a FAISS-Based Retrieval Pipeline: A Controlled Comparison of Twelve Configurations over Philippine SEC Online-Lending Records
Author
Audrey Benjamin Gutierrez
Course Week
Week 15 · 19–24 October 2026
Format
IEEE-format technical report, PDF, 6 pages

The central artifact of my Finals work. It compares twelve chunking configurations, a 4×3 grid of chunk size (300, 500, 1000 and 1800 characters) and overlap ratio (0, 10 and 20 percent), on the cleaned Member 3 corpus. Nine questions were broken into eighteen evidence units, each located by its exact text before any configuration was run, and every configuration was scored with Precision@3, Recall@3 and MRR@3.

The 1800-character configurations tied for the best scores (Recall@3 0.685, MRR@3 0.667), and the report recommends 1800 characters with 180 of overlap. It is also clear about what the data cannot settle: exact sign tests separate the leader from only one other configuration, and no comparison survives Bonferroni correction. Two findings hold up better. The rule used to judge relevance changes the scores more at small chunk sizes, and every configuration below 1000 characters leaves some evidence that no chunk holds whole.

Optimizing RAG Parameters and Comparing CPU and GPU Performance for LendSuri

Title
Optimizing RAG Parameters and Comparing CPU and GPU Performance for the LendSuri Online Lending Platform Chatbot
Authors
Stephen Rainiel Adame, Mica Ellah Mae Nerido, and Audrey Benjamin Gutierrez (QuantumStack)
Course Week
Week 16 (26–31 October 2026) · submitted 4 November 2026
Format
IEEE-format experiment report, PDF, 3 pages

The group experiment combines all three members’ datasets into one 379-page corpus and tests three chunking configurations on nine fixed questions, three from each member. Each configuration was run on a High-RAM Colab CPU and on an NVIDIA L4 GPU, with two timed trials per question and a correct-and-grounded score for each answer.

On the GPU every configuration answered in about 11 seconds, while the CPU took between 256 and 486 seconds, and the default free-tier CPU did not finish a single answer in about 40 minutes. The report recommends 1,200-character chunks with 200 overlap on the L4, and it states plainly that the CPU and GPU accuracy differences cannot be blamed on the hardware alone.

Finals Reflection

Final Project Learning Reflection

My contributions to the team project, the skills I gained, and my key personal takeaways from the Finals period.

I was Member 3 of QuantumStack, so my main job was the Member 3 dataset: 126 pages of SEC records on lending companies and online lending platforms. When I started the chunking experiment I noticed that every page repeated the same header, page number and disclaimer, which added up to about a fifth of the text, so I cleaned those out before testing anything. I also checked the evidence for the three Member 3 questions and wrote six more of my own. For WW-F1 and WW-F2 we didn’t split the work. The three of us combined our datasets, ran the tests on the CPU and the L4 GPU, checked the answers and wrote the report together.

By the end of the Finals I could build a retrieval pipeline from a PDF all the way to an answer: cleaning the text, chunking it, embedding it, indexing it in FAISS or Chroma, and running SEA-LION on top. The part I learned most from was measuring it properly. I scored retrieval with Precision@3, Recall@3 and MRR@3 against evidence I fixed before any run, and I added significance tests because with only nine questions a single answer could move the score a lot. That is why my report doesn’t name one best chunk size, even though the 1800-character settings scored highest.

My biggest takeaway is that the chatbot can only be as good as what it retrieves. No chunk size ever found the Peso2you record, because so many other platforms have Peso, Cash or Loan in their names, and in PT-F1 the model refused instead of guessing. A better prompt can’t fix that, but a retriever that also matches exact names might. The group test taught me the practical side: the free Colab CPU never finished an answer in about 40 minutes, while the L4 GPU took about 11 seconds. If I did it again I’d write at least thirty test questions instead of nine, run three timed trials like the guide asked, and save every answer so we could explain our results later.
