ITC-C508 · Prelim to Finals

Course Reflection

Each period of ITC-C508 left me with a working system and a list of ways it could fail. This page puts those failures in the places where they would matter: a support desk, a barangay office, and a borrower holding a loan app.

Prelim · In Real Life

An airline support inbox

My Prelim classifier sorted airline customer messages into praise, complaints, and urgent support requests. Picture it running at a real support desk, sending the urgent messages to a person first. The no-code version scored 91.67% on its in-vocabulary test cases, yet it read a sarcastic complaint as Positive_Praise. A sarcastic “great service” is exactly what a frustrated passenger writes, and that message would have gone to the wrong queue.

The Keras rebuild hid a quieter problem. Five of my configurations tied on validation weighted F1, between 0.9051 and 0.9076, while their test F1-scores ranged from 0.5281 to 0.8296. If a support team had picked the wrong one of those five, nothing in the validation numbers would have warned them. With only twelve test examples I could not even say how much of that spread was noise. For a real deployment I would test on far more messages, include sarcastic and ambiguous ones on purpose, and check the urgent class on its own before trusting an overall score.

Midterm · In Real Life

A question at the barangay hall

In PT-M1 I built a chatbot over RA 9003, the solid waste law every local government unit has to follow. Picture a barangay officer asking it for the fine for littering before writing a citation. My chatbot refused, although Section 49(a) answers it, because a 500-character chunk had cut the peso amounts away from the offence they belong to. A refusal is safer than a wrong number, but the officer cannot tell “the law does not say” from “the system could not find it”.

The opposite failure worried me more. When I deleted the one clause restricting the model to the retrieved text, it answered a question about Venus from memory in all three trials, with chunks of the waste law printed beside the answer as its sources. A resident reading that output would see citations and assume the answer came from them. In a real deployment I would treat the system prompt as a tested, versioned file, and split a statute by Section instead of by character count.

LendSuri, my group’s proposal, applied the same idea to money. A Filipino borrower who finds a lending app on Facebook wants to know what the SEC has on record about it. We designed LendSuri to show dated SEC records with their sources and to say when the evidence is insufficient, instead of telling anyone that a platform is safe.

Finals · In Real Life

A borrower checking a loan app

In the Finals we built and tested LendSuri. Picture a borrower typing a question about Peso2you before downloading it. My PT-F2 experiment found that no chunk size ever retrieved the Peso2you record. The retriever kept returning other records whose names share the words Peso, Cash and Loan, such as PesoQ. That is the dangerous case in real life: a borrower asks about one app and gets evidence about a similarly named one, and our corpus includes SEC advisories about lenders whose names were used by others, such as Tala and First Digital Finance. My PT-F1 prompt made the model refuse instead of guessing, which is the right behaviour, but the fix belongs in retrieval, with a retriever that also matches exact names.

The group experiment added a limit that no prompt can solve. On the default free Colab CPU, LendSuri did not finish a single answer in about 40 minutes; on an L4 GPU it answered in about 11 seconds. A borrower deciding whether to install an app will not wait 40 minutes, so the hardware decides whether the system is usable at all. We also recorded only two timed trials and did not save the answers, so a real team could not audit why the CPU and GPU runs scored differently. On a real project I would log every response from the first run.

What I Am Taking Into Real Work

The Same Lesson, Three Times

Across all three periods the same thing kept happening. A system looked fine on the numbers I chose and failed somewhere I had not measured: in the Prelim it was a small test set, in PT-M1 a chunk boundary, and in the Finals two product names that share a word. In real use the person on the other end is a passenger, an officer or a borrower who acts on the answer.

The habits I want to keep come straight from those failures. I want to decide how I will measure before I run anything, test the cases most likely to break, and build systems that show their evidence and say plainly when they do not know.
